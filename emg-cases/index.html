Guide

How to use it

  1. Pick a patient. Read the referral: the history and examination are all you get, as in a real lab.
  2. Nerve conduction. Choose a study. The arm shows the recording electrode (dark) and the stimulation sites (yellow). Click a site to stimulate it, on the arm or beside the traces. Stimulate each site you need; the latency, amplitude and segment velocity fill in. Median and ulnar motor studies also offer F waves.
  3. Needle EMG. Click a muscle on the arm, or a deep or axial muscle from the list. The needle goes in at rest: watch and listen (turn the sound on) for insertional activity, fibrillations, positive sharp waves and fasciculations. Press Space to move the needle and sample another spot. Then ask for minimal, moderate and maximal effort to judge the motor units and their recruitment. During effort, a unit is captured in the triggered panel when its spikes cross the red trigger line: move the needle on the needle pad until one does, or press Space to jump beside another firing unit.
  4. Use the Left arm button for side-to-side comparisons. Proximal studies (musculocutaneous, axillary, MABC) are best judged against the other side.
  5. When you have enough, choose the localisation and pathophysiology and check your answer. The answer explains why a wrong choice does not fit, lists the tests that settle the case and which of them you did, and ends with a one-line pattern to remember.
  6. Marking. Localisation must match exactly. For pathophysiology, "Axon loss" is the answer when there is no focal slowing or block. When a case has focal slowing or conduction block (carpal tunnel, ulnar neuropathy at the elbow, radial neuropathy at the spiral groove), "Demyelination" and "Demyelination and axon loss" are both accepted, and the answer names the better fit.

The machine's automated needle readings are hidden at first, as when you write a report yourself. Grade each muscle in the EMG table of the report (Show report): insertional activity, fibrillations and positive sharp waves, fasciculations, motor unit amplitude, duration and polyphasia, recruitment and interference pattern. Check my answer then compares your grades with the model's. Press "Show automated readings" beside the scope to see the machine's grades instead.

Reading the screen

  • Traces are shown negative-up. The blue tick marks the onset, the dot the negative peak.
  • Motor latencies are onset latencies; sensory latencies are peak latencies. Sensory studies are antidromic, except the palmar studies (orthodromic, palm to wrist, 8 cm).
  • Values outside the reference limits are red with an asterisk. Musculocutaneous and axillary amplitudes have no fixed limit here: compare with the other side (a drop of more than half is abnormal).
  • Fibrillation grades follow the count of fibrillating fibres near the needle: 1+ a few, 4+ filling the screen.

Reference values used

Typical adult values from standard teaching texts (Preston and Shapiro), not a specific laboratory's. Real limits depend on age, height and temperature.

How the model works, and what it leaves out

  • The lesion sits on the brachial plexus simulator's wiring: each muscle and each sensory nerve is reached by a route from root to trunk, division, cord and nerve, with the same root weights. A lesion removes, blocks or slows the axons that pass through it.
  • Root lesions are preganglionic: they remove motor axons but spare the sensory axons, so SNAPs stay normal even where the skin is numb.
  • Conduction studies come from the EMG & NCS simulator's axon population (160 motor and 200 sensory axons with their own velocities). Focal demyelination slows a stretch of one segment; conduction block stops a fraction of the axons in a segment.
  • Needle EMG comes from the same simulator's muscle model: 40 motor units in a cross-section, with denervation, collateral sprouting and reinnervation over time. Every muscle uses the same generic model, so size differences between muscles (for example deltoid versus a hand muscle) are not shown.
  • Wallerian degeneration is assumed complete. Studies done in the first week or two after an injury, when distal stimulation can still look normal, are not modelled.
  • Each hand muscle has the same root weights, so the split hand of ALS and the APB-predominant wasting of thoracic outlet syndrome cannot be shown. Fascicular sparing (for example FCU in cubital tunnel lesions) is not modelled.
  • Insertional activity, temperature, age and stimulus intensity are idealised: every stimulus is supramaximal.
  • The clinical vignettes are written by hand to match the lesion; they are teaching cases, not real patients.
