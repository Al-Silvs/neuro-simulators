Pick a scenario, then read the study the way you would in the lab. Tick Predict first to hide the interpretation until you have made your own call.

Reading the traces

Click ⚡ on a site, here or on the nerve, to send an impulse in slow motion. The gain stays fixed, so a smaller trace is a smaller response.

Blue tick = onset; blue dot = negative peak; the blue bar under each trace is the negative-peak duration, from onset to where the waveform returns through baseline (the measure the EAN/PNS dispersion criterion uses). Red * marks an abnormal value; D marks a demyelinating-range value (distal latency above 130% of ULN, or velocity below 75% of LLN).

F waves: twelve stimuli at the wrist. The grey M wave is not to scale; the blue line marks the shortest F latency, the dashed red line the upper limit.

SNAP: Stimulate the sensory axons at the wrist and record with ring electrodes on the digit (antidromic). Onset latency gives the velocity (distance ÷ onset latency); peak latency is read at the negative peak; amplitude here is baseline to negative peak (many labs report peak to peak).

EAN/PNS 2021 criteria: the criteria need abnormalities in at least two nerves (or one nerve plus another demyelinating feature); this simulator studies one nerve at a time. Conduction block counts only when the distal CMAP is at least 20% of LLN.

Muscle cross-section

Press Space (or "Insert needle") to move the needle a little and re-insert it: you see the burst of insertional activity, then the spontaneous activity at the new spot. EMG screen and motor unit

The upper screen sweeps 100 ms at a time (10 ms/div); the strip below shows the same 2 seconds at 200 ms/div, so waxing, waning and bursts are easier to see. At rest the gain is 50 µV/div.

The motor unit panel superimposes six consecutive discharges (blue) over their average (dark). Sweeps that do not line up mean an unstable unit: immature sprouts with jitter and blocking. Duration is measured where the average first leaves and finally returns to a ±12 µV band around baseline. Those small initial and terminal phases are invisible at the gain that shows the spike, so the panel also draws the average in orange at 20 µV/div, the gain at which duration is read in the lab. The dashed red lines mark where it crosses the band.

How the models work, and what they leave out

Nerve

  • The nerve holds 160 motor and 200 sensory axons, each with its own conduction velocity (motor about 42–59 m/s). A CMAP is the sum of one waveform per motor unit, shifted by that axon's arrival time; a SNAP is built the same way from shorter, smaller sensory waveforms. Slowing, dispersion, phase cancellation and block all come from that sum, nothing is drawn by hand.
  • The nerve picture shows 8 representative motor axons (largest and fastest at the top) and 6 sensory axons drawn from the same population the traces use, chosen so the share drawn intact matches the share of the whole population intact. Clicking ⚡ sends one impulse per axon at that axon's own speed, slowed 110× for motor and 300× for sensory. It travels both ways: orthodromically to the recording electrode, and antidromically (faint) towards the cord. From the wrist, two motor axons send an F wave (purple) back to the muscle.
  • Axon loss removes axons. With "largest axons lost first" the fastest go first, which slows conduction a little but never into the demyelinating range (velocity below 75% of LLN, distal latency above 130% of ULN).
  • Patchy demyelination slows each axon by a different amount and blocks the worst-affected, which produces conduction block and temporal dispersion. Uniform demyelination slows every axon by the same proportion, so responses stay synchronous.
  • Transected axons conduct normally distal to the cut until Wallerian degeneration: motor responses fall from about day 3 and are gone by day 9; sensory responses fall from about day 6 and are gone by day 11 (Chaudhry and Cornblath, Muscle & Nerve 1992). A cut in the root is preganglionic, so sensory axons keep their cell body in the dorsal root ganglion and the SNAP survives.
  • EAN/PNS 2021 electrodiagnostic criteria for CIDP are checked for this one nerve. The criteria need findings in two nerves, and a few thresholds differ for the tibial nerve and for median neuropathy at the wrist.
  • Reference limits are typical adult values from Preston and Shapiro's tables; each laboratory sets its own. Temperature, age and height are not modelled. Sensory conduction is only measured wrist to digit, so sensory sparing across a proximal motor block (as in MMN) is described but not shown.

Muscle

  • About 1,100 fibres belong to 40 motor units in a mosaic, as in normal muscle. A motor unit potential is the sum of every fibre's potential at the needle: a sharp spike that falls off steeply with distance (fibres within about 0.5 mm set the amplitude) plus a broad far-field component (more distant fibres set the initial and terminal phases, so they set the duration).
  • When an axon is lost its fibres are denervated. From week 2 they fibrillate; denervated fibres shrink and become angular, and after about 18 months without reinnervation they are replaced by connective tissue. From about week 5 neighbouring surviving units sprout and adopt them, most within 4 months. New sprouts are slow and unreliable, which makes the potential polyphasic and unstable from shot to shot. Over about 3 months they mature, leaving large, long-duration, stable potentials and fibre-type grouping.
  • Recruitment follows the size principle. Firing rate rises with drive, so when units are missing the remaining ones fire faster before the next is recruited (reduced recruitment). In myopathy each unit generates less force, so more units are needed for a small force (early recruitment).
  • Fibrillation onset depends on distance from the lesion (about 10–14 days near, up to 3–6 weeks for distal muscles after root lesions); the model uses a single 2-week onset. Numbers are qualitative: amplitude and duration depend on muscle, age and needle type, so compare against the normal model rather than fixed limits.
  • Each needle placement (Space, a drag, or a new scenario) adds a burst of insertional activity from fibres the needle injures. The burst is longer when fibres are denervated or necrotic, and shorter when fibres have been replaced by connective tissue. In myotonia, needle movement provokes the myotonic discharges, which then die away; insert again to provoke more.
  • Not modelled: end-plate noise and spikes, nascent units after complete denervation (regrowth from the proximal stump), single-fibre jitter measurement, neuromuscular junction disorders, and the mixed small-and-large units of chronic myopathy.
